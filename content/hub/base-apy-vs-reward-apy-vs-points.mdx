Nearly every yield figure you see quoted is a sum of up to three things that behave completely
differently. Pulling them apart is the whole skill.

## Base APY: interest

Base APY is money that borrowers pay. Someone wanted to borrow against your deposit, they are
paying a rate for it, and a share of that rate reaches you. It exists for as long as borrow
demand exists, and it needs no budget from anyone.

On tokenized stock markets, base APY is currently close to zero nearly everywhere, because
there is almost no borrow demand for tokenized equities.

## Reward APY: a marketing budget

Reward APY is the protocol paying you in its own token to attract deposits. It is an expense on
someone's budget, and it ends when the budget ends or the token price falls. A 20% reward APY
and a 20% base APY are not comparable products — one is a trade with a borrower, the other is a
promotion.

Two questions make the difference concrete:

1. **Who is paying, and out of what?** If the answer is "the protocol, out of its treasury,"
   you are being paid to show up, not to lend.
2. **What happens if the programme stops tomorrow?** For base APY, nothing. For reward APY,
   your yield goes to zero that day.

## Points: a lottery ticket

Points are a tally of your deposits that may convert into a future airdrop. No rate, no
valuation, no date. There is nothing wrong with farming them deliberately, but a points
programme cannot be expressed as an APY without inventing a number, so we never do.

## How to read any rate in ten seconds

- Find the base/reward split. If a site will not show it, that is itself the answer.
- Ask whether the venue lends your asset out at all. Collateral-only venues pay 0% by design —
  see our [glossary](/glossary#collateral-only).
- Check whether the strategy borrows. A vault that borrows against your deposit has a
  liquidation price; a plain deposit does not.
- Check the TVL. A high rate on a tiny pool usually will not survive your deposit arriving.
